Sunscreen or Bug Spray First? The Correct Order

the direct, evidence-based answer for wait time, DEET, reapplication, kids and sensitive skin

By Filip · Founder and editorial researcher, Stylish & Healthy

Published: July 25, 2026 | Last updated: July 25, 2026

This article is not medically reviewed. It was fact-checked against public-health guidance, regulatory documents and peer-reviewed studies and is intended for education only.

Quick answer

Apply sunscreen first. When possible, let it set for at least 15 minutes, then apply bug spray or insect repellent second. Put repellent only on exposed skin and the outside of clothing as directed. Reapply sunscreen according to its own label; reapply repellent only as often as its label permits.14

Bug spray or sunscreen first? Sunscreen goes first

If you are wondering whether bug spray or sunscreen goes on first, the answer is straightforward: apply sunscreen first and insect repellent second. CDC guidance recommends separate products in that order.12

  1. Apply sunscreen generously to dry, exposed skin. Use a broad-spectrum sunscreen and cover commonly missed areas such as the ears, neck, feet and backs of the hands.8
  2. Allow the sunscreen to set. When possible, wait at least 15 minutes before applying repellent, especially when the sunscreen label directs application before sun exposure.4
  3. Apply insect repellent second. Use only enough to cover exposed skin and the outside of clothing. Do not spray it directly into the face; apply it with the hands while avoiding the eyes and mouth.26
Why separate products matter

Sunscreen and repellent have different application amounts and reapplication schedules. Applying them separately lets you maintain sun protection without repeatedly applying more repellent than the label allows.

How long should you wait between sunscreen and bug spray?

When possible, wait at least 15 minutes after sunscreen before applying insect repellent. The exact instruction on the sunscreen label takes priority. Mineral-only products may not require a pre-sun delay, but they still need even coverage and should be allowed to settle before another product is placed over them.4

In a hurry?

Apply the sunscreen as evenly as possible, let it stop feeling wet or easily movable, then apply the repellent gently rather than rubbing aggressively. Correct order and adequate sunscreen coverage matter more than chasing an exact minute.

How to reapply sunscreen and bug spray during a real outdoor day

Sunscreen and insect repellent do not run on the same clock. During prolonged sun exposure, CDC guidance allows sunscreen to be reapplied over an earlier repellent layer. Repellent should then be reapplied only when the label permits it or when protection has ended.4

If you are outside for less than two hours

  1. Apply sunscreen.
  2. Wait at least 15 minutes when possible.
  3. Apply repellent to exposed skin and the outside of clothing.
  4. Wash treated skin after returning indoors.

If you are outside all afternoon

  1. Start with sunscreen first and repellent second.
  2. Reapply sunscreen at least every two hours, and according to the label after swimming, sweating or towel drying.8
  3. Do not automatically reapply repellent every time you reapply sunscreen.
  4. If the repellent needs reapplication, let the new sunscreen layer settle first and then apply repellent as directed.

If you are swimming or sweating

U.S. water-resistant sunscreen labels specify either 40 or 80 minutes of protection while swimming or sweating. Reapply after that period, immediately after towel drying and otherwise at least every two hours.9

  • Use a water-resistant broad-spectrum sunscreen.
  • Rebuild the sunscreen layer first after water exposure.
  • Apply repellent afterward only if its label allows another application.
  • Do not apply repellent to skin underneath clothing.
  • Apply repellent to the outside of clothing only when the product label permits it.

Does DEET make sunscreen less effective?

DEET can reduce measured SPF, but it does not completely cancel sunscreen. CDC describes the evidence as limited and says DEET-containing repellent applied over sunscreen may reduce SPF by roughly one-third.2

A small 1997 study reported an approximately 33% reduction in measured SPF when DEET repellent and sunscreen were used sequentially.12 A later clinical trial primarily examined whether sunscreen affected repellent performance and referenced the earlier SPF finding.11 These studies do not prove that every modern sunscreen-DEET pairing loses exactly one-third of its protection.

DEET, picaridin, IR3535 and PMD with sunscreen

EPA-registered skin-applied repellents can contain DEET, picaridin, IR3535, oil of lemon eucalyptus, PMD or 2-undecanone. Registered repellents are evaluated for safety and effectiveness when used according to their labels.7

Should you use a sunscreen and insect repellent combination product?

Separate products are usually the better choice. Sunscreen is applied liberally and frequently. Repellent is applied only in the amount and frequency specified on its label. FDA's 2021 proposed sunscreen order discussed this conflict and proposed that sunscreen-repellent combinations not be considered generally recognized as safe and effective because their directions for use are difficult to reconcile.10

Best practice

Use one sunscreen and one insect repellent. This lets you reapply sunscreen without repeatedly dosing yourself with more repellent than necessary.

Kids, pregnancy, acne, eczema and sensitive skin

Children

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends sunscreen first and insect repellent second when separate products are used.16 Adults should apply repellent for young children and keep it away from children's hands, eyes, mouth, cuts and irritated skin.17

  • Do not spray repellent directly on a child's face.
  • Apply facial repellent to adult hands first, then spread it carefully.
  • Do not apply repellent to a child's hands.
  • CDC recommends no OLE or PMD for children under three.
  • Use hats, long sleeves, stroller nets and shade to reduce product exposure.

Pregnancy and breastfeeding

CDC states that EPA-registered repellents are considered safe and effective for pregnant and breastfeeding people when used as directed.1 In areas with mosquito-borne disease, skipping effective protection can carry its own risk.

Acne-prone skin

Use repellent only where insect protection is needed. Favor exposed body skin and clothing when practical, avoid heavy facial application and cleanse gently after exposure.

Eczema, rosacea or sensitive skin

Do not apply repellent over cuts, wounds, active dermatitis, sunburn or other irritated skin.6 Fragrance-free products may be easier to tolerate than heavily fragranced alternatives, but no repellent is guaranteed not to irritate.

Sensitive-skin strategy

Use clothing first, sunscreen on exposed intact skin second and a fragrance-free EPA-registered repellent only where needed. A small-area test may identify immediate irritation, but it cannot guarantee tolerance during sweating, sunlight or repeated use.

Should bug spray go before or after makeup?

There is little direct evidence defining the ideal sequence with makeup. For substantial insect exposure, the most defensible order is sunscreen, wait, carefully applied facial repellent and then minimal makeup when practical. Makeup placed between or over the protective products may disturb either layer.

  1. Apply skincare and sunscreen.
  2. Allow the sunscreen to set.
  3. Apply repellent carefully to exposed facial areas. Spray into the hands first and avoid the eyes, lips and mucous membranes.2
  4. Keep makeup light when insect exposure is significant.
  5. For sunscreen reapplication, use lotion, cream or a properly applied stick. Do not rely on a light dusting of SPF powder or makeup alone to reproduce labeled protection.

Practical answer: in intense sun or heavy mosquito exposure, prioritize adequate sun and bite protection over preserving perfect makeup.

Can sunscreen and bug spray interact on the skin?

Laboratory studies have found that some DEET-and-oxybenzone combinations increased the in-vitro permeation of both ingredients. Results depended on the formulas, amounts and application sequence tested.13 A separate human-epidermis study found lower permeation with picaridin than DEET under the tested conditions.15

These experiments show that formulation interactions are possible. They do not establish that ordinary label-directed use causes clinical harm. EPA's review supports registered DEET products when used as directed.5

Do not overinterpret laboratory evidence

Use separate products, avoid overapplication, keep repellent off damaged skin and wash treated skin when protection is no longer needed. There is no basis for describing normal label-directed use as automatically toxic.

Does mineral or chemical sunscreen change the order?

No. Sunscreen still goes first and repellent second. Mineral and organic-filter sunscreens both need even coverage across exposed skin.

Sunscreen and bug spray routine by activity

Normal summer walk

  • Broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher on exposed skin.
  • Wait at least 15 minutes when possible.
  • EPA-registered repellent where insects are a problem.
  • Wash treated skin when you return indoors.

Hiking, camping, travel or outdoor work

  • Use water-resistant SPF 30 or higher.
  • Add a hat, sunglasses, long sleeves and shade.
  • Apply repellent after sunscreen has set.
  • Reapply sunscreen on its schedule.
  • Reapply repellent only according to its label.
  • Consider correctly used permethrin-treated clothing in tick-heavy settings.3

Frequently asked questions

Should sunscreen or bug spray go first?

Apply sunscreen first. When possible, let it set for at least 15 minutes, then apply insect repellent.

Do you put bug spray or sunscreen on first?

Sunscreen goes on first and bug spray goes on second. Use separate products.

How long after sunscreen can I apply bug spray?

When possible, wait at least 15 minutes, while following both product labels.

Can you put bug spray over sunscreen?

Yes. That is the order recommended by public-health guidance.

Can you put sunscreen over bug spray?

Yes, when sunscreen needs reapplication during prolonged exposure. Reapply repellent afterward only when its label permits it.

Does DEET make sunscreen less effective?

Limited evidence indicates that DEET can reduce measured SPF. It does not eliminate all sunscreen protection.

Can kids use sunscreen and repellent together?

Yes, with age-appropriate products, adult application and avoidance of the hands, eyes, mouth, cuts and irritated skin.

Should I use a sunscreen-repellent combination?

Separate products are generally better because sunscreen usually needs more frequent application than repellent.
